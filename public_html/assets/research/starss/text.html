STARSS

Read the paper: Volpato et al. 2023
Whether a protein is free, bound or part of a large assembly changes how fast it rotates. Time-resolved fluorescence anisotropy measures this rotation, but because fluorescence lasts only nanoseconds it can only follow small molecules, far below the size of most protein complexes in the cell. With selective time-resolved anisotropy with reversibly switchable states (STARSS) we overcome this limit. Instead of the short-lived fluorescent state, we use the long-lived ON and OFF states of reversibly switchable fluorescent proteins, the same proteins we use for RESOLFT nanoscopy. Polarized light switches a subset of molecules with a defined orientation, and we follow how this orientation is lost over microseconds to milliseconds. This extends the observable mass range by more than three orders of magnitude, up to complexes such as ribosomes and viral capsids.

Because STARSS works with genetically encoded labels, it can be applied directly in living cells. We used it to measure the rotational mobility of chromatin, to tell apart immature and mature HIV-1 virus-like particles from the rigidity of their Gag lattice, and to detect oligomers of the synaptic protein Arc. STARSS can also be combined with RESOLFT, so anisotropy and super-resolution images are recorded together.
